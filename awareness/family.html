Raising Digital Natives

Security Awareness · July 2026 · Ioannis Konstas

Kids don't experience "going online." There is no boundary to cross; the internet is simply where their friendships, games, and homework already live. Security advice built on fear — never talk to strangers, the internet is dangerous — fails because it contradicts their daily lived experience. What works is the same thing that works for crossing streets: skills, practiced early, adjusted by age.

The threat model, honestly

Parents worry most about predators; statistically, kids are far more often hurt by scams (free game currency, fake giveaways), account theft (a "friend" asking for their login), cyberbullying, and oversharing that resurfaces years later. Grooming is real and serious — but it typically follows a recognizable script: an online-only friend who escalates intimacy, asks for secrecy, moves the chat to private platforms, and requests images. Teach the script, not the fear.

Skills by age

Young children (roughly under 10)

Co-play and co-browse. Devices live in shared spaces. Use the platform's kids mode and app-store approval, not because filters are perfect but because they buy you time to teach. The core lesson at this age is simple: if anything online feels weird, you come get me, and you will never be in trouble for it.

Tweens

First phones and first accounts. Set up together: strong passwords in a family password manager, MFA on the email, privacy settings reviewed side by side. Agree on the rules explicitly — what gets shared, what gets asked first — and put parental controls in the open, never as surveillance discovered later. Secret monitoring, once found, spends trust you cannot easily rebuy.

Teens

Shift from control to consultation. Talk about digital permanence (colleges and employers do look), sextortion (it targets boys as often as girls; paying or complying always escalates; telling a parent always helps), and the economics of "free" — if the app costs nothing, the product is attention and data. A teen who understands the incentive structure of the internet is safer than one who memorized rules.

House rules that actually hold

  • The no-blame guarantee. Kids hide problems they expect to be punished for. The family deal: report anything scary immediately, amnesty included.
  • Gaming chat = public. Anything said in voice or text chat is said to the whole internet (see the gaming guide).
  • No account sharing, ever — not with best friends, not "just to borrow a skin." Most kid account theft starts as a favor.
  • Charging happens outside bedrooms. Boring, ancient, still the single most effective screen-time control ever invented.
  • Parents follow the rules too. Ask before posting photos of your kids; their digital footprint starts with yours.
Stop legislating and start rehearsing. Think in scripts, not scares. Connect with your kids online before someone else does.
