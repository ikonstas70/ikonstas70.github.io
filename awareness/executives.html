The Executive Briefing

Security Awareness · July 2026 · Ioannis Konstas

Cybersecurity is a business continuity problem wearing a technology costume. The board question is never "are we using the right firewall" — it's "how many days of downtime can we survive, what would a regulator say about our data handling, and who decides what at 3 a.m. on the worst night." Executives who internalize five ideas make their organizations measurably harder to kill.

1. You are a target because you exist

"We're too small to interest hackers" misunderstands the adversary: it's automation. Scanners probe every internet-facing system on earth continuously; ransomware affiliates work from those lists, not from Fortune rankings. Small organizations are targeted more, because they pay ransoms faster and litigate less. Meanwhile you personally — with your wire authority and your assistant who "handles your inbox" — are the highest-value phishing target in the building. Executive exemptions from security controls are how executives become the incident.

2. Most incidents are boring, and that's actionable

The breaches that make the news involve novel exploits; the breaches that happen involve a phished password, an unpatched public server, or a vendor with your data and worse hygiene. The corollary is optimistic: unglamorous controls stop the majority of real attacks — phishing-resistant MFA everywhere, aggressive patching of anything internet-facing, offline backups that get tested by restoring, and least-privilege access so one stolen credential doesn't open every door. If your security budget conversation is about AI-powered threat platforms before those four are done, reorder the conversation.

3. Incident response is a decision problem

During a ransomware event, the technology is the easy half. The hard half: Who can authorize taking production offline? Do we pay — and who decides, with counsel, insurer, and possibly sanctions law in the room? What do we tell customers, and when — noting that disclosure clocks (72 hours under GDPR; four business days for SEC-registrant material incidents) start ticking whether you're ready or not. These questions have terrible answers when first asked at 3 a.m. Write the playbook now, print it (the wiki will be encrypted too), and run a tabletop exercise twice a year. The tabletop is the cheapest security control you will ever buy.

4. Your perimeter includes companies you don't control

Vendors, MSPs, payroll processors, and software suppliers hold your data and your access. Contract for security: breach notification deadlines, MFA requirements, the right to ask hard questions annually. Know which vendors could take you down, and have a manual fallback for the top three.

5. Culture is a control

The employee who clicked isn't the failure point; the system that made one click catastrophic is. Organizations where reporting a mistake is safe detect incidents in hours instead of months — fear is a threat actor's best friend. Fund security as a percentage of what it protects, put someone clearly accountable in the room where decisions happen, and let them finish the boring work first.

The one-page checklist

  • Phishing-resistant MFA on email, VPN, admin accounts, and finance systems — no executive exemptions.
  • Tested, offline backups; a restore drill on the calendar.
  • Written, printed incident-response plan; tabletop twice a year.
  • Wire-transfer changes verified by callback on a known number — the control that kills business email compromise.
  • Vendor list ranked by blast radius, with notification clauses in contracts.
  • Cyber insurance reviewed against actual exclusions, not the brochure.
Stop budgeting for gadgets before basics. Think in downtime days, not threat feeds. Connect accountability to someone with a seat at the table.
