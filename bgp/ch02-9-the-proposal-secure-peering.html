The Proposal: Secure, Low-Exposure Peering

Chapter 2: Reading a Path · A field report from Route Views · 3 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

The findings showed what leaks and what can be forged. This part proposes the answer: a peering design built on three rules. Accept only what can be verified. Announce only what you own. Expose nothing that is not required for routing to work.

Design goals

GoalMeaningFindings it answers
Verified inEvery route accepted is checked for origin, and as standards mature, for path7, 8, 9, 10
Minimal outAnnounce only owned and customer prefixes, with nothing internal attached3, 4, 5
Hardened sessionsA BGP session can only be formed by the intended peer, and cannot be overwhelmed6, 11
Protected control planeThe router's CPU serves routing first; noise and the public never reach it11
Observed without exposureFull visibility for the operator, none for the public11, all
Deliberate decisionsExit selection and failover are policy, not tie-breakers1, 2

Peering strategy: better, not only safer

ChoiceWhy it is better
Two or more transit providers, on separate routers and sitesNo single upstream to flood or lose; the clean public-cloud block in Part 8 had six
IXP route servers with RPKI filteringMany peers in one session, with invalid routes already removed by the exchange
Private interconnects with the largest content networksShorter, more predictable paths for most traffic, as the one-hop paths to Google and Cloudflare showed
Local preference by relationship: customer > peer > transitTraffic follows the business, not the oldest session
Graceful shutdown community before maintenanceTraffic moves off a session before it drops (RFC 8326)
Join MANRSA public commitment to filtering, anti-spoofing, coordination and validation that peers can check

A configuration template

A compact FRRouting example of the import and export policy for one transit session. The same structure maps directly to IOS XR, Junos or BIRD.

! FRRouting example. Documentation addresses (RFC 5737) and ASNs (RFC 5398).
rpki
 rpki cache 10.0.0.10 3323 preference 1        ! validator A, management network
 rpki cache 10.0.0.11 3323 preference 2        ! validator B, different software
exit
!
router bgp 64500
 bgp router-id 192.0.2.1                         ! public loopback, not an internal address
 neighbor 198.51.100.1 remote-as 64510
 neighbor 198.51.100.1 description TRANSIT-A
 neighbor 198.51.100.1 ttl-security hops 1       ! GTSM: only a directly connected peer
 neighbor 198.51.100.1 password <secret>         ! TCP-AO where both sides support it
 neighbor 198.51.100.1 local-role customer strict ! RFC 9234: leak prevention
 address-family ipv4 unicast
  neighbor 198.51.100.1 route-map TRANSIT-IN in
  neighbor 198.51.100.1 route-map EDGE-OUT out
  neighbor 198.51.100.1 maximum-prefix 1200000 90
 exit-address-family
!
ip prefix-list OWN       seq 5  permit 192.0.2.0/24           ! exactly what we announce
ip prefix-list BOGONS    seq 5  permit 10.0.0.0/8 le 32       ! plus the full bogon list
ip prefix-list TOO-LONG  seq 5  permit 0.0.0.0/0 ge 25 le 32
bgp community-list standard INTERNAL permit 64500:1000      ! our informational tags
bgp as-path access-list LONG-PATH seq 5 permit (_[0-9]+){50,}
!
route-map TRANSIT-IN deny 10
 match rpki invalid                              ! origin validation
route-map TRANSIT-IN deny 20
 match ip address prefix-list BOGONS
route-map TRANSIT-IN deny 30
 match ip address prefix-list TOO-LONG
route-map TRANSIT-IN deny 40
 match ip address prefix-list OWN                ! our own space never comes from outside
route-map TRANSIT-IN deny 50
 match as-path LONG-PATH
route-map TRANSIT-IN permit 100
!
route-map EDGE-OUT permit 10
 match ip address prefix-list OWN                ! announce only what we own
 set comm-list INTERNAL delete                   ! publish nothing internal
route-map EDGE-OUT deny 100

Two lines in it carry most of the value: match rpki invalid on the way in and match ip address prefix-list OWN on the way out. Everything else narrows what can go wrong.

For organizations that do not run BGP

Most businesses never configure a BGP session; they rent addresses from cloud, CDN and access providers. The same principles still apply, one level up:

  • Choose providers by routing security: exact-length ROAs, origin validation enforced, several upstreams. All three are visible on a public collector.
  • Expose nothing you do not have to: put public services behind a CDN, keep management interfaces reachable only through a VPN, and close every port that is not serving users.
  • Watch your blocks: free alerting on the prefixes you depend on tells you about a hijack before your users do.

The takeaway

Secure peering is not one product; it is a short list of controls that each close one gap found in this chapter. Verify what comes in, announce only what you own, keep everything internal inside, and watch from the outside. Done together, they turn a router that tells the world about your business into one that tells it only where your addresses are, which is all BGP ever needed to say.
