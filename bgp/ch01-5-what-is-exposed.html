What Is Exposed, and Your Turn

Chapter 1: One Login, the Whole Internet · 3 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

Everything in this chapter is shared voluntarily. It is still a great deal of information, which is exactly why it matters.

One command deeper, show ip bgp <prefix> reveals any organization's upstream providers (and whether it is single-homed), its peering relationships, its traffic engineering (prepending shows primary and backup), its communities (often city, PoP and customer type, and blackhole tags that reveal a DDoS in progress), its more-specific routes, and its RPKI status.

What is not exposed: configurations, passwords, keys, internal routing (OSPF, IS-IS), private addressing, traffic contents or volumes. BGP carries reachability only.

Reconnaissance versus defense

An attacker uses it to…You defend by…
Find a single upstream to floodMulti-homing; DDoS scrubbing
Find prefixes with no ROA to hijackPublishing ROAs for every prefix; asking providers to filter invalid routes
Find BGP session addressesTCP-AO or MD5, GTSM (TTL security), port-179 filters, control-plane policing, maximum-prefix limits
Read communities to map PoPsStripping internal communities at the edge
Announce your space and watch it spreadMonitoring your own prefixes on public collectors, with alerting
The defender's lesson. The same public data attackers use for reconnaissance is the best free early-warning system you have. If someone hijacks or leaks your address space, it appears on these collectors within seconds.

Your turn

Log in (telnet route-views.routeviews.org, username rviews) and try:

CommandLook for
show ip bgp 8.8.8.8How many paths reach Google DNS, through whom, and with which communities
show ip bgp regexp _15169$Every prefix Google originates
show ip bgp regexp ^6939_The table as Hurricane Electric sees it
show ip bgp 1.1.1.0/24 longer-prefixesCloudflare's prefix and any more-specifics: the shape a hijack takes
show ip bgp <your own prefix>Your network as the world sees it: upstreams, prepends, communities, RPKI

Exercise. Pick three Established neighbors and compute their updates per second. Which is the noisiest, and why might that be?

The takeaway

From one public login and one command you measured the Internet (917,000 IPv4 prefixes, 20 million paths, 3.6 GB of state), its churn (over a billion best-path changes), its security (a third of paths RPKI-valid, 17,810 invalid in plain sight) and the filtering policy of the world's largest carriers.
