Leaks and Possible Hijacks, Assessed Fairly

Chapter 2: Reading a Path · A field report from Route Views · 3 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

A route with two origins is the classic signature of a hijack. It is also the signature of anycast, DDoS protection, a network migration and a typing error. Calling something a hijack is an accusation, so this part follows one rule: classify with evidence, and say plainly when the evidence is not enough.

The method

route-views> show ip bgp inconsistent-as
route-views> show bgp ipv6 unicast inconsistent-as

Both commands list every prefix that reaches the collector with more than one origin AS (a MOAS conflict). For each one we compared the RPKI state of each origin's paths, counted how many of the collector's feeds carry each origin, and checked whether the conflicting origin is simply the other origin's upstream provider. We also scanned the collected paths for the shape of a route leak: a smaller network sitting between two large carriers.

The 46 real conflicts, read one by one

In every one of them, the RPKI-valid origin is seen by almost the whole collector (median 14 feeds), and the invalid origin by almost nobody (median 1 feed). That is origin validation working: the invalid announcement exists, but the large networks that drop invalid routes do not pass it on. The patterns behind them:

Route leaks

A leak shows up as a valley in the AS path: a smaller network between two large carriers, for example a customer re-announcing one provider's routes to another. Scanning every path collected in this chapter for a large carrier, then a non-carrier, then another large carrier found no such pattern. That is a sample, not the whole table, but it is the expected result where BGP Roles, maximum-prefix limits and RPKI filtering are in place.

Is anything a deliberate hijack?

Finding 12 · Info

No evidence of an active, deliberate hijack in this snapshot

A deliberate hijack has a recognizable shape: an unrelated origin, often for a more-specific prefix, spreading through many networks. Nothing in this snapshot has that shape. Every invalid origin is either the legitimate holder's own provider, a cloud acting for a customer, or a typing error, and almost all of them were stopped after one hop.

What the data does show is plenty of misconfiguration, and RPKI containing it. The 432 conflicts with no ROA at all are the honest gap: for those, a collector alone cannot tell legitimate from malicious, and no one should claim otherwise.

Fairness in reporting

DoDo not
Call a conflict "invalid" or "unauthorized" when RPKI says soCall it a "hijack" without evidence of intent and spread
Note how many networks carry it, and through whomName a network as an attacker because it appears in a path
Check the obvious explanations first: provider, cloud, typo, migrationPublish other people's session details or addresses
Tell the prefix holder privately and give them time to fix itTreat a single snapshot as a history

Confirming intent takes more than one view: route history (when did the origin first appear?), several collectors (RIPE RIS, other Route Views collectors), registry data, and ideally a word with the operators involved.
