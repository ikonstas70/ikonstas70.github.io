Finding 1 · Info

The best path is via the University of Washington (AS 101)

Ten of the fifteen paths are two hops long (provider, then Google). With equal local preference, equal length, the same origin code and MED only comparable within one neighbor AS, the Cisco decision process falls through to the tie-breakers: the oldest external path, then the lowest router ID. Path #9 won because it has been stable longest, not because it is faster or better.

Why it matters: in your own network, if you do not set local preference deliberately, the exit your traffic takes is decided by which session flapped least recently. That is not a policy; it is an accident.

Finding 2 · Info

Google is one hop from almost everyone

Path lengths: ten paths are X 15169, five are X Y 15169. Hurricane Electric, Lumen, AT&T, Swisscom, IIJ, Coloclue and others all connect to Google directly. The three-hop paths are research and regional networks reaching Google through one upstream.

Why it matters: the content giants built their own global networks and peer directly with access and transit providers. For most destinations users care about, the "Tier 1 hierarchy" is no longer the path.

Finding 3 · Medium

Lumen's communities are a public map of its business

The path via Lumen carries 3356:3 3356:86 3356:576 3356:666 3356:901 3356:2012. Lumen publishes the meaning of its informational communities: a region value (3356:3 for North America, 3356:2 for Europe), country and city or PoP codes, and a relationship tag. On this route 3356:666 marks a route learned from a peer; on Cloudflare's route (Part 3) Lumen adds 3356:123, its customer tag.

Why it matters: from one public line you learn that Google peers with Lumen in North America while Cloudflare buys transit from it. Communities tell you where a route entered a carrier and on what commercial terms. Check the current Lumen community guide before relying on any single value.

Finding 6 · High

The router does not understand Large Communities, and passes them on anyway

unknown transitive attribute: flag 0xE0 type 0x20 is attribute type 32, the Large Community (RFC 8092). This collector's software shows it as raw hex. Decoding it, 12 bytes per community:

0000 E20A | 0000 0065 | 0000 00C8   ->  57866:101:200
0000 E20A | 0000 0066 | 0000 A1E1   ->  57866:102:41441
0000 E20A | 0000 0067 | 0000 0001   ->  57866:103:1
0000 E20A | 0000 0068 | 0000 001F   ->  57866:104:31

They mirror the standard communities on the same path (65102:41441, 65103:1, 65104:31): AS 57866 tags its routes both ways. Coloclue (AS 8283) does the same: 8283:0:1, 8283:5:1, 8283:8:26.

Why it matters: flag 0xE0 means optional, transitive, partial. BGP's rule is that a router must forward an optional transitive attribute it does not recognize. That rule is what lets new features spread, and it is also how a single malformed attribute has crossed many networks and reset sessions far from its source. Your edge should be running code that handles unknown attributes gracefully (RFC 7606, "treat-as-withdraw"), and you should know whether it does.

One more attribute

The path through AS 12552 carries Extended Community: 0x43:100:0. Type 0x43 is a non-transitive extended community; the format matches the origin-validation-state community of RFC 8097, which one network uses to tell its own routers the RPKI result. It is meant to stay inside that network. Seeing it here suggests it leaked across the boundary: harmless on a collector, but a sign of an edge that does not scrub internal markings.
