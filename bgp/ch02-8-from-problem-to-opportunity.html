Five Years of Progress, and the Opportunity

Chapter 2: Reading a Path · A field report from Route Views · 3 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

Every finding in this chapter is public. That is the uncomfortable part, and it is also the opportunity: the same visibility that helps an attacker plan lets every defender measure, fix and verify, for free, in minutes.

A caution before the conclusion: the peer mix changed (fifteen full feeds now, twenty-two then), so absolute path counts are not directly comparable. The shares are, and over those five years they moved decisively: the IPv4 table grew by more than 200,000 prefixes while the share of paths with no ROA fell from two thirds to under a third. Where networks publish ROAs and their providers drop invalid routes, invalid announcements stop spreading. RPKI is not a theory any more; you can watch it work from a public login.

The opportunity, in four sentences

  1. Visibility is symmetric. What an attacker reads on a collector, you can read first, and every hijack or leak of your space appears there within seconds.
  2. The fixes are cheap. A ROA takes minutes in the registry portal. Stripping communities and aggregator data is a few lines of policy. Monitoring is free.
  3. The enforcement is already built. Lumen, AT&T, Hurricane Electric, Arelion, NTT and most large carriers drop RPKI-invalid routes. Publish a correct ROA and the world's largest networks enforce it for you, at no cost.
  4. The gaps are measurable. Every finding here can be checked before and after a fix with the same public command. Few security controls are that easy to prove.

Proof that the fixes work: three clean blocks

The same session checked three blocks that serve ordinary websites and cloud servers: a CDN block, a static-site hosting block and a public-cloud block. All three are what good looks like.

BlockOperatorPathsRPKIMore-specificsUpstreams visible
104.21.32.0/20Cloudflare (13335)16all validnonedirect peering with almost every network
185.199.110.0/24Fastly, serving GitHub Pages (54113)15all valid(a /24: none possible)direct peering, plus Internet2, RETN, Liberty Global
150.136.0.0/16Oracle Cloud (31898)15all valid, maxLength 16noneArelion, NTT, Lumen, Cogent, GTT, Internet2

The Oracle block is the model case. Its ROA authorizes exactly the /16, at exactly length 16. If anyone announced a /24 inside it, every origin-validating carrier would mark it invalid on length alone and drop it. The block is also reachable through six independent upstreams, so there is no single provider to flood. A small business renting servers inside a block like this inherits that protection without owning an AS or writing a single ROA.

The lesson for organizations that do not own address space: you inherit your providers' routing security. Ask them three questions before you buy: Do you publish exact-length ROAs for the blocks I will use? Do you drop RPKI-invalid routes? How many upstreams carry my block? The answers are visible on a public collector, so you can check them yourself.

What still remains, and what comes next

Origin validation answers one question: is this origin allowed to announce this prefix? It does not check the path. A forged-origin announcement of an exact-length prefix (pretending to be the real origin) still validates. Two newer standards close that gap and are worth tracking now:

TechnologyWhat it addsStatus
ASPA (Autonomous System Provider Authorization)Each network publishes its legitimate upstream providers; routers reject paths that could not have happenedIETF draft with validator and router support emerging; publish ASPA objects as registries offer them
BGP Roles and OTC (RFC 9234)Sessions declare customer, peer or provider; an Only-To-Customer attribute stops route leaks automaticallyStandard; supported in several open-source and commercial routers
RPKI-based route-server filtering at IXPsExchange route servers drop invalid routes for all members at onceWidely deployed at major exchanges

The takeaway

The routing table is the most honest security report on the Internet: anyone can read it, nobody can edit it, and it updates every second. Read once, it lists what is exposed. Read twice, it shows that the exposure shrinks wherever operators act. Each fix in this chapter costs minutes to days, and the result is enforced by the largest networks on the planet. That is the opportunity. Part 9 turns it into a concrete design.
