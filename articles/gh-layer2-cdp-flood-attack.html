The CDP Flood: A Layer 2 Denial-of-Service Demo and How to Defend Against It

Articles · September 2026 · Ioannis Konstas

Summary. Layer 2 attack tools abuse the protocols switches trust by default — CDP, STP, DTP, DHCP, 802.1Q, HSRP, VTP and more. This note walks through a lab demonstration of one of the simplest and most effective of them: a Cisco Discovery Protocol (CDP) flood. In the recording below, a single host fills a switched network with thousands of forged CDP neighbours in seconds and drives the routers’ CPU up as they struggle to process them — a denial of service built entirely out of a “harmless” discovery protocol. Then we cover what actually stops it.

1. The lab

The demonstration runs in an isolated virtual lab built on VMware ESXi, with Cisco CSR 1000V routers (R1–R10) and a Catalyst switch, reached over SSH, and a Linux host running a Layer 2 attack tool attached to the same segment (interface ens160). Nothing here touches a production network. That matters, and we come back to it at the end.

2. What CDP is — and why it is a target

The Cisco Discovery Protocol is a Layer 2 protocol that Cisco devices use to announce themselves to their direct neighbours: device ID, model, IOS version, the port they are connected through, native VLAN and more. It is convenient for mapping a network — and for the same reason it is a gift to an attacker. CDP frames are sent to the multicast address 01:00:0C:CC:CC:CC, they are not authenticated, and by default a switch believes every one it receives.

Before the attack, the neighbour table is small and real — the lab switch and routers R1–R10:

R7# show cdp neighbors

3. The attack: flooding CDP

The attack tool offers a “flood CDP table” function. One action and the host begins emitting a torrent of CDP frames, each with a randomly generated device ID and source MAC address. Within moments the neighbour table is full of devices that do not exist:

The CDP table after a few seconds of flooding
TTL   DevID      Interface   Count   Last seen
FF    BPPPPPP    ens160      1       24 Dec 12:04:17
FF    YYYYYYY    ens160      1       24 Dec 12:04:17
FF    TTTBBBB    ens160      1       24 Dec 12:04:17
FF    K222222    ens160      1       24 Dec 12:04:17
FF    NNNNNNN    ens160      1       24 Dec 12:04:17
...
It is extremely rapid. Notice the timestamps — every forged neighbour above arrives in the same second (12:04:17). The frames are emitted thousands per second, so the table fills and the network starts to suffer almost the instant the attack begins. There is no slow build-up to notice and react to; it is immediate.
It is very hard to trace. Every forged frame carries a random source MAC and a random device ID, and each one looks like a perfectly ordinary CDP advertisement. On a switch with many access ports, nothing in the neighbour table tells you which physical port the flood is entering through — the entries do not point back to a source. Without per-port packet captures, interface-level CDP counters, or port security already in place, localising the attacker among dozens of live ports is slow and painful — exactly when you have the least time to spare.

4. The impact

CDP processing happens in software on the route processor. Flooding thousands of unique advertisements forces each device to parse, store and age them continuously, and the cost shows up directly as CPU. In the demonstration, the routers’ CDP Protocol process climbs from idle to a large share of the CPU:

R1 — CPU consumed by the CDP Protocol process during the flood
R1# show processes cpu sorted | include CPU | PID Runtime | CDP Protocol
CPU utilization for five seconds: 68%/13%; one minute: 53%; five minutes: 22%
 PID  Runtime(ms)   Invoked      5Sec     1Min     5Min   Process
 570  73245         300          41.54%   48.65%   19.70%  CDP Protocol

On real hardware with many ports and neighbours, a sustained flood can starve the control plane of CPU — delaying or dropping the legitimate control traffic that keeps the network converged (routing adjacencies, spanning tree, management). That is the denial of service: not a dramatic crash, but a network slowly choking on fake discovery data.

5. The defence

CDP flooding has a clean answer: CDP is almost never needed on an access port, and where it is not needed it should be off. Everything else is defence-in-depth around the same idea.

Turn CDP off where it is not needed

Cisco IOS — disable CDP globally, or per edge port
! Disable CDP entirely (if nothing depends on it)
Switch(config)# no cdp run

! Or leave it on for infrastructure links, off on access/edge ports
Switch(config)# interface range Gi1/0/1 - 48
Switch(config-if-range)# no cdp enable

In the demonstration, this is exactly the lever that is pulled — no cdp run on the routers, after which show cdp neighbors reports “CDP is not enabled” and the flood has nothing to land on.

Harden the access edge so one port cannot do this

ControlWhat it stops
Port security — switchport port-security maximum 2, violation shutdownCaps the number of MAC addresses a port may learn. A host spraying thousands of random source MACs trips the violation and the port is err-disabled — which also tells you exactly where the attack is.
BPDU Guard — spanning-tree bpduguard enableShuts a port the instant it sees a rogue BPDU, killing spanning-tree attacks.
DHCP Snooping + Dynamic ARP InspectionStops DHCP starvation / rogue-server and ARP-spoofing attacks from the same class of toolkit.
Disable DTP — switchport mode access, switchport nonegotiatePrevents DTP negotiation and 802.1Q VLAN hopping.
Storm control — storm-control broadcast level 1.00Caps any flood that slips past the controls above.
Cisco IOS — a hardened access-port baseline
interface range Gi1/0/1 - 48
 switchport mode access
 switchport nonegotiate
 no cdp enable
 spanning-tree portfast
 spanning-tree bpduguard enable
 switchport port-security
 switchport port-security maximum 2
 switchport port-security violation shutdown
 switchport port-security aging time 2
 storm-control broadcast level 1.00
The auditor’s test. Plug a test machine into an access port and run the flood. With this baseline in place, the port should err-disable within seconds — which both stops the attack and pinpoints the source port in your logs. If the port instead stays up and the neighbour table fills with strangers, you have your finding.

6. Why this matters beyond the lab

Layer 2 is the floor the whole network stands on, and it is frequently the least-hardened layer because the protocols involved are old, chatty and “just work.” Attack tools exist to prove that those defaults are a liability. The lesson is not the tool — it is that every trust a switch extends by default is a control you have to make a conscious decision about: which ports speak CDP, how many MACs a port may learn, whether a port may become a trunk, who is allowed to be the root bridge. Decide each one on purpose, and the whole class of attacks closes.

The convenience trap. In practice, most networks leave CDP enabled everywhere — it is on by default, it makes troubleshooting and network mapping easier, and IP phones lean on it for voice-VLAN and PoE negotiation, so administrators simply never turn it off. At the same time, buildings are full of live wall jacks — in lobbies, reception areas, conference rooms, empty offices, behind printers — that are patched straight back to access switches which uplink to the core switches and routers. Put those two together and the picture is uncomfortable: anyone who can reach a network jack — an insider, a contractor, a visitor left alone in a meeting room — is one cable away from launching this attack against the core, with CDP obligingly enabled to receive it. The flaw is rarely the protocol alone; it is a trusted protocol left on for convenience, reachable from an untrusted physical port. Disable CDP where it is not needed, and treat every accessible jack as hostile until port security, 802.1X and the rest of the baseline prove otherwise.
Authorisation, not enthusiasm. These are disruption and denial-of-service techniques. Run them only on equipment you own, in an isolated lab, or under a written, scoped engagement. Pointing them at a network you do not own is a crime in the United States (CFAA) and almost everywhere else — and the “it’s a security tool” argument is no defence. Authorisation is the entire line between a penetration tester and an attacker.
