Block Zero to Block 974,612: Reading Bitcoin from Its First Breath

Article · 4 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

000000000019d6689c085ae165831e934ff763ae46a2a6c172b3f1b60a8ce26f

“The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks”

The hash of block zero, and the sentence its creator chose to carry inside it forever.

Every bank keeps a ledger, and every one of those ledgers asks you to trust the bank. Bitcoin keeps a ledger too, except anyone on Earth can read every line of it, from the very first entry written in January 2009 to the block that was mined a few minutes ago. This article reads that ledger directly: the first block, the second, the first payment between two people, and the accounting that has balanced to the last satoshi for seventeen years.

Nothing on this page is taken on faith. Every number below was pulled live from the public Bitcoin network with a handful of small scripts, which are printed at the end so you can run them yourself. That is the spirit of the system: don't trust, verify.

1 · Block 0: the Genesis Block

On 3 January 2009, at 18:15:05 UTC, a person or group writing as Satoshi Nakamoto created the first block of a chain that would grow to secure more value than many national economies. It is called the Genesis Block, and it sits at height zero. Here is what the network still returns for it today:

The message in the machine

A coinbase transaction has no real input to spend, so its input field is free space. Satoshi used it to write the front-page headline of The Times of London from that very morning: “Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.”

The headline does two jobs. Technically, it is a timestamp: the block cannot have been created before that newspaper was printed, which proves Satoshi did not secretly mine a head start. Philosophically, it is a statement. In the middle of a banking crisis paid for by the public, the first entry in a new kind of money pointed straight at the old kind.

A small detail for the curious: when you decode the raw input, a stray letter E appears just before “The Times”. It is not part of the message. It is the byte 0x45, which means “the next 69 bytes are data”, and 0x45 happens to be the letter E in ASCII. Our scripts read the script push by push, so they print only the headline.

The 50 bitcoins that can never be spent

The Genesis Block's reward exists, but it can never move. The block is written directly into the Bitcoin software rather than received from the network, and its transaction was never added to the database of spendable coins. Whether that was deliberate or an accident of the original code, the result is a permanent monument: 50 BTC locked at the very root of the chain. People still send small tributes to that address, where they will most likely stay.

2 · Block 1: the first link in the chain

That six-day silence has never been fully explained. Some think Satoshi was testing, others that the Genesis Block was prepared in advance and its timestamp set to match the headline. What matters is the result: from block 1 onward, every block carries the hash of the one before it. Change a single byte anywhere in history and every hash after it breaks. That is the whole security model of Bitcoin in one sentence.

3 · The accounting of the 50

Bitcoin's ledger runs on two simple rules, and every block since 2009 has obeyed both:

Rule 1 (normal transactions) inputs = outputs + fee Rule 2 (the coinbase) new coins (subsidy) + all fees in the block = miner reward

There are no account balances in Bitcoin. There are only coins, called unspent outputs, and each one is spent whole, like a banknote. To pay less than a coin is worth, you spend all of it and send yourself the change. Here is the money supply growing through the first blocks:

Block 170: the first payment from one person to another

For the first nine days, from the Genesis Block on 3 January to 12 January, coins were only created, never sent. Then, on 12 January 2009 at 03:30:25 UTC, transaction f4184fc596403b9d638783cf57adfe4c75c605f6356fbc91338530e9831e9e16 was confirmed in block 170. Satoshi took the 50 BTC mined in block 9 and sent 10 of them to Hal Finney, the cryptographer who had been the first to download and run the software. It was the first time value moved from one person to another on the Bitcoin network.

INPUT 50.00000000 BTC coinbase of block 9 (key 0411db93e1dcdb8a…) OUTPUTS 10.00000000 BTC to Hal Finney (key 04ae1a62fe09c5f5…) 40.00000000 BTC change, back to sender (key 0411db93e1dcdb8a…) CHECK 50.00000000 = 10.00000000 + 40.00000000 + fee 0.00000000 balanced

The pattern is right there in the first payment ever made: spend the whole coin, pay the recipient, return the change to yourself. Every wallet in the world still works this way. The fee was zero because there was no competition for space in a block. Today it never is.

The halving: scarcity written in code

The 50 BTC subsidy is not permanent. Every 210,000 blocks, about every four years, it is cut in half. No committee votes on it and no central bank can change it. The rule is enforced by every node that checks every block.

Add up the eras and the total never quite reaches 21 million coins. The last fraction of a bitcoin will be created around the year 2140. After that, miners will be paid by fees alone.

4 · The same ledger, tonight

Seventeen years later, the rules have not changed by a single satoshi. Here is block 974,612, mined by F2Pool at 01:47:27 UTC on 5 November 2026 (20:47 the previous evening in New York), read and checked on the evening this article was written:

Look at the hash. The Genesis Block needed 10 leading zeros. Block 974,612 has 19. The difficulty has risen from 1 to about 148 trillion, because the whole world now competes for each block with specialised machines. That rising wall of energy is what makes rewriting history so expensive that no one tries.

Block times are random. Block 974,611 arrived just 10 seconds after 974,610, and then the network waited almost 18 minutes for 974,612. The difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks so the average stays at ten minutes, but every individual block is a lottery draw.

What you can and cannot see

Watching transactions arrive live in the mempool, the waiting room before a block, you sometimes see a single transfer of 50, 73 or 2,482 BTC go by. But the blockchain records movements between keys, not purchases or names. When someone buys bitcoin on an exchange, the exchange usually just updates its own internal database, and nothing appears on the chain until coins are withdrawn or moved. Bitcoin is transparent about what moved, and silent about who moved it.

5 · The code: query it yourself

No node, no account, no API key. Everything above comes from the public mempool.space REST API, with Google BigQuery's public Bitcoin dataset for historical analysis. Here are the scripts behind this article.

One-line queries with curl

# The Genesis Block header (JSON)
curl -s https://mempool.space/api/block/000000000019d6689c085ae165831e934ff763ae46a2a6c172b3f1b60a8ce26f | python3 -m json.tool

# The hash of any block by height
curl -s https://mempool.space/api/block-height/1

# The transactions in a block
curl -s https://mempool.space/api/block/000000000019d6689c085ae165831e934ff763ae46a2a6c172b3f1b60a8ce26f/txids

# Has block 1's 50 BTC ever been spent?
curl -s https://mempool.space/api/tx/0e3e2357e806b6cdb1f70b54c3a3a17b6714ee1f0e68bebb44a74b1efd512098/outspends
# [{"spent":false}]

# The current tip of the chain
curl -s https://mempool.space/api/blocks/tip/height

block_info.sh: a readable view of any block

#!/bin/zsh
# Usage: zsh block_info.sh [height or hash]
python3 - "${1:-0}" <<'EOF'
import sys, json, urllib.request, datetime as d
A = 'https://mempool.space/api'
get = lambda p: urllib.request.urlopen(A + p, timeout=30).read().decode()
b = sys.argv[1]
h = b if len(b) == 64 else get(f'/block-height/{b}')
blk = json.loads(get(f'/v1/block/{h}'))
tip = int(get('/blocks/tip/height'))
line = lambda k, v: print(f'  {k:<22} {v}')
print(f'\n  BITCOIN BLOCK {blk["height"]:,}\n')
line('Block hash', blk['id'])
line('Previous block', blk.get('previousblockhash') or 'none (genesis block)')
line('Merkle root', blk['merkle_root'])
line('Time', f'{d.datetime.fromtimestamp(blk["timestamp"], d.UTC):%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S} UTC')
line('Confirmations', f'{tip - blk["height"] + 1:,}')
line('Bits / difficulty', f'{blk["bits"]}  /  {blk["difficulty"]:,.0f}')
line('Nonce', f'{blk["nonce"]:,}')
line('Transactions', f'{blk["tx_count"]:,}')
EOF

tx_info.sh: inputs, outputs and the hidden message

#!/bin/zsh
# Usage: zsh tx_info.sh <txid>
python3 - "$1" <<'EOF'
import sys, json, urllib.request
A = 'https://mempool.space/api'
tx = json.loads(urllib.request.urlopen(f'{A}/tx/{sys.argv[1]}', timeout=30).read())
print('\n  INPUTS')
for i, v in enumerate(tx['vin']):
    if v.get('is_coinbase'):
        s = bytes.fromhex(v['scriptsig']); j = 0; parts = []
        while j < len(s):                  # read the script push by push:
            n = s[j]; j += 1               # a length byte, then that many data bytes
            if 1 <= n <= 75: parts.append(s[j:j+n]); j += n
        msg = ' '.join(p.decode() for p in parts if len(p) >= 4 and all(32 <= c < 127 for c in p))
        print(f'  #{i}  coinbase (new coins)   {msg}')
    else:
        p = v['prevout']
        print(f'  #{i}  {p["value"]/1e8:14.8f} BTC   spends {v["txid"]}:{v["vout"]}')
print('\n  OUTPUTS')
for i, o in enumerate(tx['vout']):
    print(f'  #{i}  {o["value"]/1e8:14.8f} BTC   {o["scriptpubkey_type"]}')
print(f'\n  fee {tx["fee"]} sats')
EOF

Run zsh tx_info.sh 4a5e1e4baab89f3a32518a88c31bc87f618f76673e2cc77ab2127b7afdeda33b to see the Times headline, or zsh tx_info.sh f4184fc596403b9d638783cf57adfe4c75c605f6356fbc91338530e9831e9e16 to see the first payment to Hal Finney balance to the satoshi.

The accounting check: parsing a whole block locally

Rather than make thousands of requests, the live feed downloads each new block once (about 1.6 MB of raw bytes) and decodes every transaction on the local machine. The miner's reward is read straight from the coinbase outputs, and the subsidy is computed from the halving rule:

subsidy = (50 * 100_000_000) >> (height // 210_000)   # in satoshis: halves every 210,000 blocks
reward  = sum(value for value, script in coinbase_outputs)
fees    = reward - subsidy                             # everything the miner earned above new coins

print(f'New coins (subsidy)  {subsidy / 1e8:.8f} BTC')
print(f'+ Transaction fees   {fees / 1e8:.8f} BTC')
print(f'= Miner reward       {reward / 1e8:.8f} BTC')

The transaction ID is computed the same way Bitcoin itself does it: a double SHA-256 of the transaction without its witness data, with the byte order reversed for display.

import hashlib
def txid(base_bytes):
    return hashlib.sha256(hashlib.sha256(base_bytes).digest()).digest()[::-1].hex()

Google BigQuery: the whole history in SQL

Google publishes the full Bitcoin blockchain as a public dataset, bigquery-public-data.crypto_bitcoin. The first terabyte of queries each month is free. Always filter on the monthly partition column to keep scans small.

-- The Genesis Block
SELECT number, `hash`, timestamp, nonce, bits, transaction_count
FROM `bigquery-public-data.crypto_bitcoin.blocks`
WHERE timestamp_month = '2009-01-01' AND number = 0;

-- The first person-to-person payment, output by output
SELECT t.block_number, o.index, o.value / 1e8 AS btc
FROM `bigquery-public-data.crypto_bitcoin.transactions` t, UNNEST(t.outputs) o
WHERE t.block_timestamp_month = '2009-01-01'
  AND t.`hash` = 'f4184fc596403b9d638783cf57adfe4c75c605f6356fbc91338530e9831e9e16';

-- Total new coins and fees per day for a month
SELECT DATE(timestamp) AS day, COUNT(*) AS blocks, SUM(transaction_count) AS txs
FROM `bigquery-public-data.crypto_bitcoin.blocks`
WHERE timestamp_month = '2026-10-01'
GROUP BY day ORDER BY day;
# Run from a Mac with the Google Cloud SDK; --dry_run shows the bytes scanned first, at no cost
bq query --use_legacy_sql=false --dry_run 'SELECT ...'
bq query --use_legacy_sql=false --format=pretty 'SELECT ...'

6 · Why it still matters

Block 0 holds a headline about banks being rescued with public money. Block 1 holds nothing at all, just the first link of a chain. Block 170 holds ten bitcoins passing from one person to another with no bank, no clearing house and no permission. And block 974,612, mined tonight, holds 2,622 transactions that balance to the last satoshi under the same rules written in 2009.

Most systems ask you to trust them. This one hands you the ledger and the tools to check it. You do not need to believe a word of this article. Run the scripts, ask the network, and watch it answer.

“The root problem with conventional currency is all the trust that's required to make it work.” — Satoshi Nakamoto, 2009
