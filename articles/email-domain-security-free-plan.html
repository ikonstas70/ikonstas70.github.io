Email Security

Locking Down Three Domains on a Free Plan

Articles · November 2026 · Ioannis (John) Konstas

This is a field report from one afternoon of hardening three business domains that all run on a free DNS and CDN plan with hosted mail. Nothing here costs money, every change is reversible, and every change was verified from the outside, the way the rest of the internet sees it. All names below are documentation examples (example.com, example.net, example.org); none of them is the real system.

Why you should care

  • Money. Forged invoices and fake messages from executives (business email compromise) cost organizations billions of dollars every year, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center.
  • Delivery. Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook now expect bulk senders to publish DMARC. Domains without it are more likely to see their real mail land in spam.
  • Trust. One convincing fake sent in your name can cost you a client relationship.
  • Cost. Everything in this report is free.

The problem: anyone can send as you

SMTP was designed without proof of sender. The From: line is plain text, so any server on the internet can claim to be billing@example.com. Unless the domain publishes a policy, the receiving server has to guess. The goal is simple: tell every mail server exactly what to do with mail that is not really from you.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC: three records, one job

  • SPF lists the servers allowed to send for the domain, ending in -all (everything else fails).
  • DKIM signs every outgoing message; receivers verify the signature against a public key in DNS.
  • DMARC ties both to the domain in the From: line and says what to do on failure. adkim=s and aspf=s require an exact match, closing the look-alike-subdomain loophole.

What the audit found

A read-only DNS audit of the three domains showed three different states: one already at p=quarantine, one at p=none (forgeries reported but still delivered), and one with no DMARC record at all. DMARC has three levels, none (report only), quarantine (spam folder) and reject (refuse delivery). Both weak domains moved to quarantine; reject follows after a week of clean aggregate reports.

_dmarc.example.org.  TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:reports@example.com; fo=1; adkim=s; aspf=s"
  • p=quarantine: failing mail goes to the spam folder.
  • rua=: daily aggregate reports from the large mailbox providers.
  • fo=1: report any single mechanism failure, not only total failure.

Verify from the outside

Dashboards show what you typed. A public resolver shows what the internet sees. After each change, the same question went to Google Public DNS:

https://dns.google/query?name=_dmarc.example.org&rr_type=TXT

"Status": 0 /* NOERROR */,
"AD": true,
"data": "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:reports@example.com; ..."

NOERROR means the name exists, "AD": true means the answer was DNSSEC-validated (signed and untampered), and data is the exact record every mail server now sees.

CAA: who may issue certificates

A CAA record set is an allow-list of the certificate authorities that may issue certificates for the domain. Any authority not on the list must refuse, so a rogue or tricked CA cannot mint a valid certificate for your name. Two of the three domains had none. The list was copied from the domain whose automatic certificate renewals already worked, so renewal keeps working:

example.net.  CAA  0 issue     "letsencrypt.org"
example.net.  CAA  0 issue     "pki.goog"
example.net.  CAA  0 issue     "digicert.com"
example.net.  CAA  0 issue     "sectigo.com"
example.net.  CAA  0 issue     "ssl.com"
example.net.  CAA  0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"
example.net.  CAA  0 iodef     "mailto:reports@example.com"

MTA-STS: stopping the downgrade attack

Server-to-server mail is encrypted only if both sides agree (STARTTLS). An attacker on the network path can strip that offer, and the message silently falls back to plain text. MTA-STS publishes a rule: deliver to this domain over TLS, to these MX hosts only, or not at all. The policy is a four-line file served over HTTPS from mta-sts.<domain>; a small edge worker serves it for all three domains, so there is no server to patch:

version: STSv1
mode: testing
mx: *.mail.protection.outlook.com
max_age: 86400

Two TXT records announce the policy and ask senders for daily TLS reports:

_mta-sts.example.com.    TXT  "v=STSv1; id=20261106T1230"
_smtp._tls.example.com.  TXT  "v=TLSRPTv1; rua=mailto:reports@example.com"

It starts in testing mode: senders report problems but still deliver, so nothing can break. After about two weeks of clean TLS reports the mode moves to enforce and the id is bumped.

Edge hardening: four small switches

  • 0-RTT resumption off. Resumed TLS early data can be replayed, which matters for form posts and APIs. The speed cost is negligible.
  • Browser integrity check on for every zone, then tested with a browser, a command-line client, a media player and a link-preview bot (all returned 200).
  • Managed ruleset left always on; it replaces the retired security-level setting.
  • API tokens: fourteen down to two. Only the two tokens actually in use remain. Every unused token is a spare key to your DNS.

Prove nothing broke

The same read-only audit script ran again after the work: MX, SPF, both DKIM selectors, DMARC, DNSSEC, CAA, MTA-STS and TLS-RPT all passed on all three domains, the websites answered 200, and the provider's audit log listed every change with its time.

The checklist

  • DMARC: quarantine now, reject after a clean week of reports; strict alignment.
  • CAA: copied from a domain whose renewals already work; iodef to a monitored mailbox.
  • MTA-STS: testing first, enforce later; policy served from the edge.
  • TLS-RPT: daily reports to the same mailbox.
  • Edge: 0-RTT off, integrity check on, managed rules on, unused API tokens deleted.
  • Verify: every change confirmed with a public resolver, and the audit re-run at the end.
Free, verifiable and reversible: publish the policy, check it from the outside, and tighten only after the reports say it is safe.
