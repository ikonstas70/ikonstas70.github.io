Building a Universe in a Computer

Investigating Cosmological Simulations & Cosmic Evolution · 5 November 2026 · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

Video · 4 min 05 s

No one can rerun the universe, so cosmologists build one. This video follows a cosmological simulation from the Big Bang to the present day: how a slight excess of matter over antimatter, and an invisible scaffolding of dark matter, shaped the cosmic web; how black holes and supernovae keep galaxies from growing out of control; and the open questions about dark matter and dark energy that drive the search across the universe.

Why simulate a universe

Astronomy has one universe and no laboratory. A telescope sees each galaxy at a single moment, never its whole life. A simulation fills that gap: it starts from the conditions recorded in the cosmic microwave background, where the young universe was smooth to about one part in a hundred thousand, then applies the laws of physics step by step for 13.8 billion years. If the universe that comes out looks like the one telescopes see, the theory that went in has passed a demanding test.

A universe made of matter

The first ingredient is an asymmetry. The early universe produced matter and antimatter in almost equal amounts, and when they met they annihilated. For roughly every billion antiparticles there were a billion and one particles. That tiny surplus survived, and it became every star, planet and person. Why nature tipped the balance at all is still one of physics' open questions.

The dark scaffolding

Most of the matter in the universe is dark: it gives off no light and is felt only through gravity. Because it does not interact with radiation, dark matter began to clump first, pulling the tiny early ripples into sheets, filaments and dense knots. Ordinary gas fell into that framework later, and where it gathered, galaxies were born. The result is the cosmic web: bright filaments and nodes surrounding vast, nearly empty voids.

The video zooms through this structure in three steps. A megaparsec is about 3.26 million light-years, and h, close to 0.7, scales the distances to the expansion rate of the universe.

Black holes and supernovae: the regulators

Gravity alone makes galaxies too big. Early simulations that followed only gravity and cooling gas turned far too much of that gas into stars. Real galaxies are held in check by feedback. In small galaxies, exploding stars, supernovae, heat the surrounding gas and blow part of it away. In the largest galaxies, the supermassive black hole at the centre releases enormous energy as it feeds, heating gas and shutting down star formation. Modern simulations include both effects as "subgrid" models, physics too small to resolve directly, and tuning them is what lets simulated galaxies match the sizes, masses and colours of real ones.

The open challenges

QuestionWhere it stands
What is dark matter?Its gravity is measured on every scale, but no experiment has yet detected a dark-matter particle directly. The search spans underground detectors, particle colliders and sky surveys.
What is dark energy?It drives the accelerating expansion of the universe. Recent large surveys hint that it may change over time rather than stay constant, a possibility the next generation of observations will test.
How fast is the universe expanding?Measurements of the expansion rate from the early universe and from nearby galaxies disagree, the so-called Hubble tension.
Do small galaxies match?On the smallest scales, simulated dwarf galaxies and their dark-matter cores do not always match what is observed, a test of both feedback models and the nature of dark matter.

Simulations are the bridge between theory and the sky. Each new survey maps millions of galaxies, and each one is compared with universes built in a computer. Where they agree, the theory holds; where they do not, the next discovery begins.

In one sentence. A slight excess of matter, an invisible web of dark matter and the regulating force of black holes and supernovae are enough to build a universe that looks like ours, and the parts that still do not fit point to where physics goes next.
