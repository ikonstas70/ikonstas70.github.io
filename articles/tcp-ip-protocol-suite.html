TCP/IP: The Protocol Suite Behind the Internet

Articles · October 2026 · Ioannis (John) Konstas

How a defence research experiment became the common language of every network on Earth, and who keeps its standards today.

Overview

The Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP) is the suite of networking protocols that carries communication across wide area networks and the Internet. Its development began in the late 1960s and early 1970s with the U.S. Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA, later DARPA). ARPA's pioneering experiments in packet-switched networking led to the creation and standardisation of TCP/IP, which has since become the backbone of modern networking.

The suite in four layers

TCP/IP is usually described in four layers, each relying on the one below it:

LayerJobExamples
ApplicationServices that people and programs useHTTP, DNS, FTP, Telnet, SMTP
TransportEnd-to-end delivery between programsTCP (reliable, ordered), UDP (lightweight)
InternetAddressing and routing between networksIP, ICMP
Network accessMoving frames over the local mediumEthernet, Wi-Fi

Key milestones

  • 1970 — ARPANET hosts adopt the Network Control Protocol (NCP), the network's first host-to-host protocol.
  • 1972 — The first Telnet specification is published as RFC 318, enabling remote terminal access.
  • 1973 — The File Transfer Protocol is specified in RFC 454, building on the first FTP proposal of 1971 (RFC 114).
  • 1974 — Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn describe the Transmission Control Program, and RFC 675 specifies it in December, laying the foundation of TCP/IP.
  • 1981 — RFC 791 defines the Internet Protocol (addressing and routing) and RFC 793 defines TCP, the versions still in use today.
  • 1982 — The Defense Communications Agency and ARPA establish TCP/IP as the standard protocol suite for military networking.
  • 1983 — On 1 January, ARPANET switches from NCP to TCP/IP, the official adoption of the suite. The Domain Name System is specified the same year (RFCs 882 and 883).
  • 1984 — DNS begins to replace the single shared hosts file, simplifying hostname resolution as the network grows.

Microsoft TCP/IP and enterprise networking

Microsoft built TCP/IP into Windows NT from its first release, and with Windows NT 4.0 it became the core protocol of the enterprise Windows network. That brought several advantages:

  • Universal compatibility. TCP/IP is a standardised, routable protocol supported across modern operating systems and enterprise networks.
  • Cross-platform connectivity. Standard utilities such as FTP and Telnet move data and provide remote access between very different systems.
  • Scalability and robustness. The Windows Sockets (Winsock) interface lets developers build client/server applications that talk to any TCP/IP system.
  • Internet access. As the Internet's own protocol suite, TCP/IP connects research institutions, government agencies and enterprises worldwide.

Who governs the Internet's protocols

No single organisation owns or controls the Internet. Its protocols and standards are developed and coordinated by several bodies:

Internet Society (ISOC)

Founded in 1992, ISOC promotes the open development, evolution and use of the Internet worldwide, and provides the organisational home for the standards process.

Internet Architecture Board (IAB)

The IAB oversees the Internet's technical architecture and the standards process. It dates from the 1980s and was chartered as part of ISOC in 1992. It oversees:

  • Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), which develops and maintains Internet protocols.
  • Internet Research Task Force (IRTF), which pursues longer-term research on protocols and future networking.
  • Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA), which manages protocol parameters, IP address allocation and the DNS root. The IAB provides policy direction for the protocol registries; since 1998 the IANA functions have been performed under ICANN.

Requests for Comments and Internet standards

TCP/IP standards are published as Requests for Comments (RFCs), which set out the technical specifications and operational guidance for Internet protocols. The process is open and consensus-based, captured in the IETF's motto of "rough consensus and running code", rather than decided by a formal committee vote.

Requirement levels

Specifications carry a requirement level that says how they apply to implementations:

LevelMeaning
RequiredMust be implemented by all TCP/IP-based systems.
RecommendedStrongly encouraged for widespread adoption.
ElectiveOptional, but recognised as useful.
Limited useIntended for specialised applications.
Not recommendedGenerally discouraged.

Maturity levels

Before it becomes a full standard, a specification passes through stages of review and real-world use. The classic standards track had three:

  1. Proposed Standard: a stable, well-reviewed specification with initial implementation.
  2. Draft Standard: widely implemented, interoperable and proven in practice.
  3. Internet Standard: fully mature and widely deployed, recognised for its significant contribution to the Internet.

Since 2011 (RFC 6410) the track has two levels: Proposed Standard and Internet Standard. The Draft Standard stage was retired.

Every RFC keeps its number permanently. When a specification needs to change, a new RFC is published that updates or obsoletes the old one, giving clear versioning and a complete historical record.

Conclusion

TCP/IP remains the cornerstone of modern networking, supporting enterprise communication, cross-platform interoperability and global connectivity. Its continued evolution, guided by open standards and international organisations, will keep shaping the future of digital communication.
