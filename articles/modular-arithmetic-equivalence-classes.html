Complete Reference Guide: Modular Arithmetic & Equivalence Classes

Articles · September 2026 · Ioannis Konstas

A comprehensive mathematical reference guide covering modular arithmetic principles, congruence relations, equivalence classes modulo 8, algebraic consistency, representative invariance proofs, and modular multiplicative inverses.

1. Core Principles & Definitions

Modular arithmetic operates on the remainders of division by a fixed positive integer called the modulus ($m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$).

  • Divisibility Notation ($x \mid y$): Read as “$x$ divides $y$,” meaning there exists an integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $y = kx$ (with $x \neq 0$).
  • Congruence Relation: Two integers $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo $m$, written as:
    Congruence Relation $$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b)$$
    Two integers are congruent modulo $m$ if and only if their difference $(a - b)$ is an exact integer multiple of $m$ (i.e., there exists $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $a - b = km$).
  • Equivalence Class (Congruence Class): For a given modulus $m$, the set of all integers that leave the identical remainder $r \in \{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$ upon Euclidean division by $m$. A modulus $m$ partitions the entire set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ into exactly $m$ pairwise disjoint, non-overlapping equivalence classes: $$\mathbb{Z} = [0]_m \cup [1]_m \cup [2]_m \cup \dots \cup [m-1]_m$$
  • Algebraic Consistency: Addition, subtraction, and multiplication are well-defined under modular equivalence. If $a \equiv a' \pmod m$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod m$, then:
    • $(a + b) \equiv (a' + b') \pmod m$
    • $(a - b) \equiv (a' - b') \pmod m$
    • $(a \times b) \equiv (a' \times b') \pmod m$

2. Equivalence Classes for Modulo 8

For modulus $m = 8$, the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is partitioned into $8$ distinct equivalence classes, uniquely indexed by their canonical remainder $r \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7\}$:

Representative Equivalence Classes Modulo 8 ($m = 8$)

Set A ($r \equiv 0 \pmod 8$): $\{\dots, -24, -16, -8, 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, \dots\}$

Set B ($r \equiv 1 \pmod 8$): $\{\dots, -23, -15, -7, 1, 9, 17, 25, 33, 41, \dots\}$

Set C ($r \equiv 2 \pmod 8$): $\{\dots, -22, -14, -6, 2, 10, 18, 26, 34, 42, \dots\}$

Set D ($r \equiv 3 \pmod 8$): $\{\dots, -21, -13, -5, 3, 11, 19, 27, 35, 43, \dots\}$

Set E ($r \equiv 4 \pmod 8$): $\{\dots, -20, -12, -4, 4, 12, 20, 28, 36, 44, \dots\}$

(Note: The remaining classes for remainders $r = 5, 6, 7$ follow the identical arithmetic progression $\{8k + r \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$).

3. Worked Proofs: Algebraic Invariance of Class Representatives

To demonstrate that modular evaluation is strictly invariant under the choice of class representative, we examine two worked evaluations.

Example 1: Multi-Class Mixed Evaluation — $19 \times 26 - 11 \pmod 8$

We map each input term to its residue class modulo 8:

  • $19 = 8 \times 2 + 3 \implies 19 \in \mathbf{\text{Set D}} \quad (r = 3)$
  • $26 = 8 \times 3 + 2 \implies 26 \in \mathbf{\text{Set C}} \quad (r = 2)$
  • $11 = 8 \times 1 + 3 \implies 11 \in \mathbf{\text{Set D}} \quad (r = 3)$

Method 1 — Canonical Representatives (Smallest Non-Negative Remainders)

Substitute each term with its canonical remainder from $\{0, 1, \dots, 7\}$:

Canonical Evaluation $$19 \times 26 - 11 \equiv (3) \times (2) - (3) \pmod 8$$ $$(3 \times 2) - 3 = 6 - 3 = \mathbf{3} \pmod 8$$

Method 2 — Alternative Small Class Elements (Signed / Offset Representatives)

Substitute distinct, small alternative members belonging to the same classes:

  • For $19 \in \text{Set D}$, select small negative representative $-5$ (since $-5 = 8(-1) + 3$)
  • For $26 \in \text{Set C}$, select small negative representative $-6$ (since $-6 = 8(-1) + 2$)
  • For $11 \in \text{Set D}$, select canonical representative $3$
Alternative Small Element Evaluation $$(-5) \times (-6) - (3) = 30 - 3 = 27$$

Reduce $27$ modulo 8:

Modulo 8 Reduction $$27 = 8 \times 3 + 3 \implies 27 \equiv \mathbf{3} \pmod 8$$

Method 3 — Direct Raw Integer Arithmetic Verification

Calculate the entire expression in standard integer arithmetic without intermediate reductions:

Direct Raw Verification $$19 \times 26 - 11 = 494 - 11 = 483$$ $$483 = 8 \times 60 + 3 \implies 483 \equiv \mathbf{3} \pmod 8 \quad (\text{Set D})$$

Example 2: Zero-Residue Evaluation — $16 \times 32 - 8 \pmod 8$

We analyze the terms in the expression $(16 \times 32) - 8$:

  • $16 = 8 \times 2 + 0 \implies 16 \in \mathbf{\text{Set A}} \quad ([0]_8)$
  • $32 = 8 \times 4 + 0 \implies 32 \in \mathbf{\text{Set A}} \quad ([0]_8)$
  • Subtracted term $8 = 8 \times 1 + 0 \implies 8 \in \mathbf{\text{Set A}} \quad ([0]_8)$

All terms reside in Set A ($[0]_8$).

Method 1 — Canonical Representatives ($0, 0, 0$)

Canonical Evaluation $$16 \times 32 - 8 \equiv (0) \times (0) - (0) = \mathbf{0} \pmod 8$$

Method 2 — Distinct Small Non-Zero Representatives ($8, -8, 16$)

Substitute distinct small elements from $\text{Set A}$ into the expression $(16 \times 32) - (8)$:

  • Replace first factor: $16 \to 8 \in \text{Set A}$
  • Replace second factor: $32 \to -8 \in \text{Set A}$
  • Replace subtracted term: $8 \to 16 \in \text{Set A}$
Distinct Small Representative Calculation $$(8) \times (-8) - (16) = -64 - 16 = -80$$ $$-80 = 8 \times (-10) + 0 \implies -80 \equiv \mathbf{0} \pmod 8 \quad (\text{Set A})$$

Sign Bookkeeping & Operator Isolation

In $(16 \times 32) - 8$, the subtraction operator is external to the term. Replacing the subtracted positive integer $8 \in [0]_8$ with $16 \in [0]_8$ yields $- (16) = -16$.

Equivalently, viewing the expression as addition of a negative value $(16 \times 32) + (-8)$ and replacing the term $-8 \in [0]_8$ with $-16 \in [0]_8$ gives $((8) \times (-8)) + (-16) = -80 \equiv \mathbf{0} \pmod 8$.

5. Modular Multiplicative Inverses & Division

In standard arithmetic, division by $b$ is multiplication by the reciprocal $b^{-1} = \frac{1}{b}$. In modular arithmetic over $\mathbb{Z}$, fractions do not exist. Instead, division is defined through the modular multiplicative inverse.

Definition

The multiplicative inverse of an integer $a$ modulo $m$ is an integer $x$ such that:

Multiplicative Inverse $$a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod m$$

We denote this element as $a^{-1} \pmod m$.

The Invertibility Condition (Coprimality)

Unlike addition and multiplication, a modular inverse does not always exist.

Theorem: Existence of Modular Inverses

An integer $a$ possesses a multiplicative inverse modulo $m$ if and only if $a$ and $m$ are coprime:

$$\gcd(a, m) = 1$$
  • If $\gcd(a, m) = 1$: The inverse exists and is uniquely determined within the canonical range $\{1, 2, \dots, m-1\}$.
  • If $\gcd(a, m) > 1$: $a$ is a zero divisor (shares a non-trivial factor with $m$). No integer $x$ can satisfy $a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod m$.

Invertibility in Modulo 8 ($\mathbb{Z}_8$)

Since $m = 8 = 2^3$, any even integer shares a common factor with 8:

Residue Class $[a]_8$ $\gcd(a, 8)$ Invertible? Inverse $a^{-1} \pmod 8$ Verification
$[0]$ 8 No — $0 \cdot x \equiv 0 \neq 1$
$[1]$ 1 Yes 1 $1 \cdot 1 = 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$
$[2]$ 2 No — $2 \cdot x \in \{0, 2, 4, 6\} \pmod 8$
$[3]$ 1 Yes 3 $3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$ (self-inverse)
$[4]$ 4 No — $4 \cdot x \in \{0, 4\} \pmod 8$
$[5]$ 1 Yes 5 $5 \cdot 5 = 25 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$ (self-inverse)
$[6]$ 2 No — $6 \cdot x \in \{0, 2, 4, 6\} \pmod 8$
$[7]$ 1 Yes 7 $7 \cdot 7 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$ (self-inverse)

The set of invertible elements forms the group of units, denoted $\mathbb{Z}_8^\times = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$. In $\mathbb{Z}_8$, every unit is its own inverse ($a^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$).

Computing Inverses: The Extended Euclidean Algorithm

To systematically find $a^{-1} \pmod m$ for large integers, we apply Bézout’s Identity:

Bézout’s Identity $$a \cdot x + m \cdot y = \gcd(a, m)$$

When $\gcd(a, m) = 1$:

$$a \cdot x + m \cdot y = 1 \implies a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod m$$

The integer coefficient $x$ computed by the Extended Euclidean Algorithm is the modular multiplicative inverse of $a$.

Worked Example: Find $17^{-1} \pmod{43}$

Step 1 — Standard Euclidean Algorithm (Find GCD):

  1. $43 = 2 \times 17 + 9$
  2. $17 = 1 \times 9 + 8$
  3. $9 = 1 \times 8 + 1 \implies \gcd(43, 17) = 1$

Step 2 — Back-Substitution (Bézout Coefficients):

Isolate remainders working backwards from 1:

$$1 = 9 - 1 \times 8$$

Substitute $8 = 17 - 1 \times 9$:

$$1 = 9 - 1 \times (17 - 9) = 2 \times 9 - 1 \times 17$$

Substitute $9 = 43 - 2 \times 17$:

$$1 = 2 \times (43 - 2 \times 17) - 1 \times 17 = 2 \times 43 - 5 \times 17$$

Step 3 — Reduction to Modulo 43:

$$-5 \times 17 \equiv 1 \pmod{43}$$

Since $-5$ is negative, reduce it into the canonical range $[0, 42]$:

$$x = -5 + 43 = \mathbf{38}$$
Verification $$17 \times 38 = 646 = 43 \times 15 + 1 \implies 646 \equiv \mathbf{1} \pmod{43}$$

Thus, $17^{-1} \equiv \mathbf{38} \pmod{43}$.

Solving Congruence Equations (Modular Division)

To solve a linear congruence equation of the form:

Linear Congruence $$a \cdot x \equiv b \pmod m$$

Multiply both sides by the modular inverse $a^{-1} \pmod m$ (provided $\gcd(a, m) = 1$):

$$x \equiv b \cdot a^{-1} \pmod m$$

Example: Solve $3x \equiv 5 \pmod 8$

  1. From the units table for $\mathbb{Z}_8$, $3^{-1} \equiv 3 \pmod 8$.
  2. Multiply both sides by $3$: $$x \equiv 5 \times 3 \pmod 8$$ $$x \equiv 15 \equiv \mathbf{7} \pmod 8$$
  3. Verification: $3(7) = 21 = 8(2) + 5 \equiv \mathbf{5} \pmod 8$.

Final Conclusion & Mathematical Principles

The calculations rigorously confirm that modular arithmetic operations are independent of the representative chosen from any equivalence class.

Core Takeaways:

  1. Class Invariance: Any integer $a$ in a given equivalence class $[a]_m$ behaves identically under addition, subtraction, and multiplication modulo $m$.
  2. Computational Efficiency: Replacing large or negative numbers with their smallest non-negative canonical remainders ($0 \le r < m$) dramatically reduces computational overhead while preserving exact mathematical correctness.
  3. Consistency Across Operations: Whether evaluating expressions with homogeneous class membership ($16 \times 32 - 8$) or heterogeneous multi-class interactions ($19 \times 26 - 11$), intermediate reductions never distort the final residue class.
  4. Inverses & Division: Modular division by $a$ is valid if and only if $\gcd(a, m) = 1$, achieved via multiplication by the modular multiplicative inverse $a^{-1} \pmod m$ computed via the Extended Euclidean Algorithm.
